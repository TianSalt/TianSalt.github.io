# 1.1 Cryptography in a Nutshell

## 1.1.1 The problem

Cryptography is the study of building secure systems in the presence of **adversaries**. Its central object is deceptively simple: Alice wants to send Bob a message over a network that an attacker — conventionally named Eve — can observe, and possibly modify. The network itself offers no protection; the only thing Alice and Bob may assume they possess is a **shared secret key** $k$, unknown to Eve.

Once such a key exists, two properties become the baseline goal:

- **Confidentiality.** Eve, who sees the traffic, cannot figure out what messages Alice and Bob are exchanging.
- **Integrity.** Eve cannot tamper with the traffic without the tampering being detected.

Confidentiality alone is not enough. A scheme that hides content but lets an attacker rearrange or flip bits in transit is like an envelope with a sealed letter whose contents can still be edited in the mail — the recipient may act on something the sender never wrote. Modern encryption schemes therefore provide both properties at once.

And before confidentiality or integrity can even be discussed, Alice and Bob must *have* a shared key. The task of getting two parties who have never met to agree on a secret key over an insecure channel — **key establishment** — is the other half of cryptography. This is why the whole subject has two parts:

> Secure key establishment, and secure communication once a shared key exists.

The first part of this course (and this tutorial) takes the shared key as a gift and asks how to use it well; the second part shows how to manufacture that gift out of thin air.

## 1.1.2 Symmetric encryption, abstracted

A **cipher** (or **encryption scheme**) is a pair of algorithms $(E, D)$ with a correctness requirement:

::: theorem Definition (Shannon cipher)
A **Shannon cipher** $\mathcal{E} = (E, D)$ defined over $(\mathcal{K}, \mathcal{M}, \mathcal{C})$ consists of

- an encryption algorithm $E : \mathcal{K} \times \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{C}$, and
- a decryption algorithm $D : \mathcal{K} \times \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{M}$,

such that for every key $k \in \mathcal{K}$ and every message $m \in \mathcal{M}$,

$$D\bigl(k,\, E(k, m)\bigr) = m.$$

Here $\mathcal{K}$, $\mathcal{M}$, and $\mathcal{C}$ are the **key space**, **message space**, and **ciphertext space**.
:::

To send a message $m$, Alice computes $c := E(k, m)$ and transmits $c$; Bob recovers $m := D(k, c)$. The cipher is **symmetric** because both parties use the same key $k$. Later we will meet *public-key* ciphers, where the encryption and decryption keys are different — that is the second half of the course.

A cipher by itself says nothing about *what security means*. Writing down $(E, D)$ is easy; arguing that $E$ actually protects $m$ against a specific class of attacks is the mathematical content. That is the discipline to which we turn in earnest in Chapter 3.

## 1.1.3 Cryptography does much more

Encryption is only the visible tip of the subject. Four families of applications recur throughout the course, and each one looks impossible until you see the construction.

**Digital signatures.** A signature is the analog of a handwritten signature, and the physical model breaks immediately in the digital world: a handwritten mark is a fixed image, so if an attacker ever obtains one signed document, he can **cut and paste** that same image onto a different document. A digital signature therefore cannot be a fixed string that the signer attaches to everything. The resolution is that a secure signature must be a **function of the message being signed**: pasting it to new content invalidates it. Construction and proofs come in the second half of the course.

**Anonymous communication.** Alice wants to talk to a chat server Bob without revealing who she is, and cryptography can do it in both directions: Bob answers Alice although he has no idea whom he is answering. The standard mechanism is a **mixnet** — a sequence of proxies through which messages are layered and peeled off with encryption, so that no proxy learns who is talking to whom. Anonymity then composes into stronger mechanisms, such as **anonymous digital cash**: Alice spends a digital coin at an online merchant without the merchant learning her identity, exactly as physical cash does today. But digital money has an obvious flaw — Alice can *copy* the coin before spending it. Anonymity seems to collide head-on with security: if a coin leaves no trace, who stops the double spender? The cryptographic trick resolves the tension with an asymmetric rule: spend the coin once and your identity stays hidden; spend it **twice** and the two spendings together reveal exactly who you are. Anonymity is granted to honest users and revoked precisely at the moment of fraud.

**Secure multi-party computation (MPC).** Consider an **election**: voters submit votes to a center, which must compute the majority while revealing nothing about any individual vote. Or a **private auction** (a *Vickrey auction*, where the highest bidder wins but pays the second-highest bid): the participants want to learn the winner and the price, but nothing about the losing bids. Both are instances of a general problem: parties hold private inputs $x_1, \dots, x_n$ and want to compute a function $f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ so that the **value of $f$** becomes public and **nothing else** is revealed. The naive “dumb” solution hands every input to a trusted authority who computes $f$ and keeps the inputs secret — but then you have built a system around a party everyone must trust. The central theorem of MPC says this authority is unnecessary:

::: theorem Theorem (secure function evaluation, informally)
Any functionality that can be computed with the help of a trusted authority can also be computed without one, by a protocol among the parties themselves, leaking nothing beyond the output.
:::

The parties talk to each other over the network, and at the end $f$ is known to everybody while each individual input remains private. The construction is far from trivial and appears near the end of the course.

**“Magic” applications.** Two more constructions have no classical counterpart at all.

- **Privately outsourcing computation.** Alice sends an *encryption* of her search query to Google; the encryption scheme has the property that Google can **run its search algorithm directly on the encrypted values**, obtain an encrypted result, and send it back — all without ever seeing the plaintext. (Such schemes are called *fully homomorphic*; they are a post-2009 discovery and were, at the time of the lectures, theoretically possible but far too slow for real search engines. The point is that “impossible” turned out to be merely “expensive.”)
- **Zero-knowledge proofs.** Alice publishes a number $N = p \cdot q$, the product of two thousand-digit primes. Factoring $N$ is believed infeasible, so Bob, who sees only $N$, cannot possibly reconstruct $p$ and $q$. Yet Alice can engage in a protocol that **convinces Bob that she knows the factorization**, while leaking *nothing* about what $p$ and $q$ actually are — and this is provable, not folklore. The phenomenon generalizes: essentially every statement that has any proof has a **zero-knowledge proof**, so you can prove you solved a Sudoku puzzle without revealing a single cell of the solution.

A common thread runs through all of the above: cryptography is the science of making *seemingly impossible* tasks possible — trusting without trusting, proving without revealing, computing without seeing.

## 1.1.4 The method: three steps

Every primitive in this course — encryption, signatures, message integrity, key exchange — follows the same rigorous template, and you will see the pattern over and over:

1. **Specify the threat model precisely.** What does the attacker control? Can she only observe ciphertexts, or can she obtain encryptions of messages of her choice? What is her goal — recovering the key, recovering one bit of a specific message, forging one signature on one chosen message? A security claim without a threat model is meaningless, because the answer changes with the model.
2. **Propose a construction.** A concrete algorithm pair, built from published, peer-reviewed components.
3. **Prove a security theorem.** “If an attacker can break this construction under the stated threat model, then there exists an algorithm that solves problem $X$ efficiently” — where $X$ is a problem the community believes to be hard (factoring, discrete logarithm, or an assumption about pseudorandomness). Proved by *reduction*: you would plug the attacker in as a subroutine to break $X$. Since we do not think $X$ is breakable, the attacker cannot exist.

This is not pedantry. History (Section 1.2) is a catalogue of beautiful-sounding ciphers that were broken because no one ever said, in advance and precisely, what “secure” was supposed to mean.

## 1.1.5 What cryptography is *not*

The slides of the course close the overview with warnings that deserve to be quoted in any textbook:

- Cryptography is **not the solution to all security problems.** It protects data in transit and at rest; it does not fix bad key management, implementation bugs, side channels, or the human at the keyboard. The famous quote from the course applies: it is the *tremendous tool* that is also the *most misused one*.
- A cryptosystem is **not reliable unless implemented and used properly.** The same AES block cipher that secures your browser was also the core of WEP, the broken scheme that protected Wi-Fi for a decade — the math was fine, the *usage* was not.
- You should **not invent your own cipher.** There are endless examples of ad-hoc designs that looked clever and fell to professional cryptanalysis within months. Use published, standardized algorithms (AES, SHA-2, TLS), reviewed by thousands of people whose job is to break them.

With the map drawn, we can begin at the beginning — with the history, because the historical ciphers are exactly the laboratory in which threat models first became necessary.
