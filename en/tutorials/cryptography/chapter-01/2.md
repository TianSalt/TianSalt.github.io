# 1.2 A Brief History of Ciphers

The best single account of this history is David Kahn’s *The Codebreakers*, which runs from Babylonian scribes to the Cold War. What follows is a compressed tour: each cipher below was *broken* by an attack that carries its own lesson, and it is those lessons that the security definitions of Chapter 2 are built to rule out.

Before the tour, we fix the characters and the notation. Alice and Bob want to communicate; an attacker listens. They share a secret key $k \in \mathcal{K}$ about which the attacker knows nothing. A **cipher** is a pair of algorithms

$$E : \mathcal{K} \times \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{C}, \qquad D : \mathcal{K} \times \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{M}, \qquad D(k, E(k, m)) = m,$$

where the ciphertext $c := E(k, m)$ crosses the hostile network. Both sides use the same key $k$, so these are **symmetric ciphers**. Two quantities matter for every historical example:

- the **key space** $\mathcal{K}$: how many possible keys exist, and
- what the attacker is given. The weakest model, a **ciphertext-only attack**, hands the attacker nothing but ciphertexts; a cipher that falls in this model is broken outright.

## 1.2.1 The substitution cipher

A **substitution cipher** hides a message by relabeling its alphabet. A key is a permutation $\pi$ of the 26 letters; encryption applies $\pi$ letter by letter. For instance, the beginning of one key might be

| plaintext | A | B | C | ⋯ | Z |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ciphertext | C | W | N | ⋯ | A |

Under this table the message `BCZA` encrypts to `WNAC`, and decryption is applying $\pi^{-1}$.

How many keys are there? A permutation of 26 letters, so

$$|\mathcal{K}| = 26! \approx 2^{88}.$$

Eighty-eight bits is a respectable key size; AES uses 128. The substitution cipher is nonetheless *terribly* easy to break, which gives the first lesson: **a large key space is necessary but not sufficient**.

## 1.2.2 The Caesar cipher: not even a key

The Roman-era **Caesar cipher** is the special case where $\pi$ is the fixed shift by three: $A \mapsto D$, $B \mapsto E$, $\dots$, $X \mapsto A$, $Y \mapsto B$, $Z \mapsto C$. There is no random choice at all: the “key” is written into the design. Anyone who knows the scheme can undo it instantly, and the design of a cipher is assumed public (Section 1.2.7). This is not a cipher in the modern sense; it is a fixed, public function. It survives here only as a cautionary tale about confusing secrecy of the *design* with secrecy of the *key*.

## 1.2.3 Breaking the substitution cipher: frequency analysis

  <img src="/src/images/cryptography/frequency-analysis-en.svg" alt="Bar chart of English letter frequencies, with E tallest, followed by T and A." width="860" class="image adaptive-image" />
</figure>

English text is not uniform over its alphabet. The letter **E** occurs roughly $12.7\%$ of the time, **T** about $9.1\%$, **A** about $8.1\%$; rare letters like Q and X sit near zero. A substitution cipher permutes letters, so it preserves each letter’s *frequency*. Hence a ciphertext-only attack on a long message:

1. Count the frequency of each ciphertext letter. The most frequent ciphertext letter is almost certainly the encryption of E; one entry of the secret table is now known.
2. Repeat for T, then A. Three entries recovered without touching the plaintext.
3. When letter frequencies alone stall (the remaining letters have similar frequencies), escalate to **digrams** (letter pairs): the most common English pairs (`TH`, `HE`, `IN`, `AN`) impose consistency constraints on candidate tables. Then **trigrams** (`THE`, `ING`, …) pin down the rest. With a few trials per level, the entire permutation $\pi$ falls, and with it every past and future message.

This is exactly a **ciphertext-only attack**: given only ciphertexts, the attacker recovers the key. The substitution cipher satisfies *no* meaningful security definition against even the weakest adversary. Chapter 2 turns “the attacker cannot recover $k$” into an experiment with a precise success probability; for the substitution cipher that probability is essentially one.

## 1.2.4 The Vigenère cipher: a good idea, badly executed

The Renaissance (16th century) brought a genuine idea: what if the substitution *changes as you go*? In the **Vigenère cipher** the key is a short word (say $k = \texttt{CRYPTO}$, of period $6$) written repeatedly under the message, and encryption is **letterwise addition mod 26**:

$$c_i = (m_i + k_{i \bmod 6}) \bmod 26 .$$

<figure class="main-figure">
  <img src="/src/images/cryptography/vigenere-en.svg" alt="The plaintext ATTACKATDAWN encrypted with the repeating key CRYPTOCRYPTOC, with the twelve positions colored in six groups." width="860" class="image adaptive-image" />
</figure>

Now the plaintext letter `E` is not encrypted to a single ciphertext letter: it is shifted by whatever key letter sits above it, so the peak at E is smeared across six positions and the letter frequencies of the ciphertext look flat. That was the point.

The execution, however, repeats the weakness of the substitution cipher: the key is *periodic*. Recover it, and the scheme collapses. The break, assuming the key length $\ell$ is known:

1. Split the ciphertext into $\ell$ interleaved subsequences: position $1, 1+\ell, 1+2\ell, \dots$ form one subsequence.
2. Each subsequence was encrypted by a *single* Caesar shift (one fixed key letter). Apply frequency analysis independently to each subsequence. The most common letter of subsequence 1 is $E$ shifted by $k_1$, so $k_1 = (\text{that letter} - \texttt{E}) \bmod 26$. Repeat for $k_2, \dots, k_\ell$.
3. If $\ell$ is unknown, run the procedure for $\ell = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ until the recovered plaintext makes sense.

Vigenère’s addition mod 26 is the right operation, and later chapters build on exactly that; what failed was *reusing* a short key. With a truly random key as long as the message, never reused, the same operation becomes the **one-time pad**, which Chapter 2 proves *perfectly* secure. What breaks a periodic cipher is the period: a short key, reused over a long text.

## 1.2.5 Rotor machines and the Enigma

The nineteenth century made ciphers electric. A **rotor machine** passes current through rotating wired substitution rotors, so each keypress applies a different substitution. The **Hebern** machine (one rotor) is the archetype: the rotor *is* the secret key, wired as a substitution table, and it advances one notch per letter: pressing `C` three times might emit `T`, `S`, `K`. For a single rotor this is just a Vigenère-style period the size of the alphabet, and it was broken, soon after being advertised, by the same frequency/digram/trigram machinery.

Rotors then multiplied in an arms race against statistics, culminating in the **Enigma**: three to five rotors, each stepping at a different rate, plus a plugboard that swapped letter pairs. With four rotors the rotor positions alone give $26^4 \approx 2^{18}$ keys; the plugboard raises the figure to about $2^{36}$, which is small by the standards of what came next.

Its undoing was not mathematics but procedure: the machines’ operators made structural mistakes (fixed message keys, repeated message beginnings, known plaintext headers), and the British cryptographers at **Bletchley Park**, building on the Polish Cipher Bureau’s earlier work, exploited these to attack Enigma traffic in World War II, an episode often credited with shortening the war. A cipher is only as strong as the *protocol and key management* wrapped around it, a theme we return to when real systems such as WEP fail in exactly this way.

## 1.2.6 DES, AES, and the block cipher

With the mechanical age ended and computers arriving, the US government issued a request for proposals for a **Data Encryption Standard**; the design submitted by IBM in 1974 became **DES**: a 56-bit key, and encryption of **64 bits at a time** (eight characters at once) rather than one character, a conceptual jump from everything above. The move from letter-level to block-level operation is what lets us escape frequency analysis, since no ciphertext block retains the frequency structure of the plaintext.

DES dominated for over two decades, but $2^{56}$ became small. By the late 1990s dedicated brute-force search was practical: the Electronic Frontier Foundation’s “Deep Crack” recovered a DES key in 1998 after about 56 hours of searching, and a few dozen modern graphics cards would repeat the search in a couple of days. **DES is considered insecure and must not be used in new systems.** Legacy systems still use it.

Its successor, standardized in 2001 and built like DES but with keys of 128 or 256 bits, is **AES**; alongside the block ciphers, the **eSTREAM** project (2008) settled on stream ciphers such as **Salsa20** for high-speed software use. Chapter 3 dissects the block-cipher design and explains why “encrypt 128 bits at a time with a 128-bit key” is the right shape of object for proofs; Chapter 2 treats the stream-cipher alternative.

## 1.2.7 What the tour has taught us

Four lessons can be read off this history:

1. **Kerckhoffs’s principle.** Assume the attacker knows the entire design; security may live only in the key. Caesar violates this in spirit; the modern formal models go further still and hand the attacker encryptions of messages she chooses.
2. **A large key space is not security.** $26!$ and $2^{18}$ both fell. What saved block ciphers was not only larger keys but *block-level operation*, which leaves a frequency analysis nothing to count.
3. **Key reuse is where attacks begin.** Vigenère falls to its period; a one-time key would not (proved in Chapter 2). The distinction between a one-time key and a many-time key runs through the first half of this tutorial.
4. **Any statistical structure in the plaintext will be exploited.** A scheme that preserves even one statistical property of its plaintexts leaks it, and the later security definitions are formal ways of saying that an attacker cannot detect any property of the plaintext beyond its length.

That vocabulary needs one more tool before it can be made precise: probability.
