# 1.2 A Brief History of Ciphers

The best single account of this history is David Kahn’s *The Codebreakers*, which runs from Babylonian scribes to the Cold War. What follows is a compressed tour, because each of the historical ciphers below was *broken* by an attack that is itself a lesson — and together the lessons force the formal definitions of Section 1.1.

Before the tour, let us fix the characters and notation once and for all. Alice and Bob want to communicate; an attacker listens. They share a secret key $k \in \mathcal{K}$ about which the attacker knows nothing. A **cipher** is a pair of algorithms

$$E : \mathcal{K} \times \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{C}, \qquad D : \mathcal{K} \times \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{M}, \qquad D(k, E(k, m)) = m,$$

where the ciphertext $c := E(k, m)$ crosses the hostile network. Both sides use the same key $k$, so these are **symmetric ciphers**. Two quantities will matter for every historical example:

- the **key space** $\mathcal{K}$ — how many possible keys exist, and
- the **attack surface** — what the attacker is given. The weakest model, a **ciphertext-only attack**, gives the attacker nothing but the ciphertexts; an attack that succeeds in this model breaks the cipher in the worst possible way.

## 1.2.1 The substitution cipher

A **substitution cipher** hides a message by relabeling its alphabet. A key is a permutation $\pi$ of the 26 letters; encryption applies $\pi$ letter by letter. For instance, the beginning of one key might be

| plaintext | A | B | C | ⋯ | Z |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ciphertext | C | W | N | ⋯ | A |

Under this table the message `BCZA` encrypts to `WNAC`, and decryption is applying $\pi^{-1}$.

How many keys are there? A permutation of 26 letters, so

$$|\mathcal{K}| = 26! \approx 2^{88}.$$

Eighty-eight bits of key is a perfectly respectable number — modern ciphers are comfortable with 128. And yet the substitution cipher is *terribly* insecure, which already teaches the first great lesson: **a large key space is necessary but not sufficient**.

## 1.2.2 The Caesar cipher: not even a key

The Roman-era **Caesar cipher** is the special case where $\pi$ is the fixed shift by three: $A \mapsto D$, $B \mapsto E$, $\dots$, $X \mapsto A$, $Y \mapsto B$, $Z \mapsto C$. There is no random choice at all — the “key” is written into the design. Anyone who knows the scheme (which by Kerckhoffs’s principle we must assume) can undo it instantly. This is not a cipher in the modern sense; it is a fixed, public function. It survives here only as a cautionary tale about confusing secrecy of the *design* with secrecy of the *key*.

## 1.2.3 Breaking the substitution cipher: frequency analysis

English text is not uniform over its alphabet. The letter **E** occurs roughly $12.7\%$ of the time, **T** about $9.1\%$, **A** about $8.1\%$; rare letters like Q and X sit near zero. A substitution cipher permutes letters but preserves every letter’s *frequency*. Hence a ciphertext-only attack on a long message:

1. Count the frequency of each ciphertext letter. The most frequent ciphertext letter is almost certainly the encryption of E — one entry of the secret table is now known.
2. Repeat for T, then A. Three entries recovered without touching the plaintext.
3. When letter frequencies alone stall (the remaining letters have similar frequencies), escalate to **digrams** (letter pairs): the most common English pairs — `TH`, `HE`, `IN`, `AN` — impose consistency constraints on candidate tables. Then **trigrams** (`THE`, `ING`, …) pin down the rest. With a few trials per level, the entire permutation $\pi$ falls, and with it every past and future message.

This is exactly a **ciphertext-only attack**: given only ciphertexts, the attacker recovers the key. The moral, in modern vocabulary, is that the substitution cipher satisfies *no* meaningful security definition against even the weakest adversary. (The textbook formalizes this by viewing “guess $k$ given $c$” as an experiment and requiring the probability of success to be negligible; the substitution cipher’s success probability is essentially one. Compare Boneh–Shoup §2.1.3, where the same attack is recast in the Shannon-cipher framework.)

## 1.2.4 The Vigenère cipher: a good idea, badly executed

The Renaissance (16th century) brought a genuine idea: what if the substitution *changes as you go*? In the **Vigenère cipher** the key is a short word — say $k = \texttt{CRYPTO}$, of period $6$ — written repeatedly under the message, and encryption is **letterwise addition mod 26**:

$$c_i = (m_i + k_{i \bmod 6}) \bmod 26 .$$

Now the plaintext letter `E` is not encrypted to a single ciphertext letter: it is shifted by whatever key letter sits above it, so the E-frequency peak is smeared across six positions. Letter frequencies in the raw ciphertext look flat. Clever.

The execution, however, repeats the sin of the substitution cipher: the key is *periodic*. Recover it, and the scheme collapses. The break, assuming the key length $\ell$ is known:

1. Split the ciphertext into $\ell$ interleaved subsequences — position $1, 1+\ell, 1+2\ell, \dots$ form one subsequence.
2. Each subsequence was encrypted by a *single* Caesar shift (a fixed key letter!). Apply frequency analysis independently to each subsequence. The most common letter of subsequence 1 is $E$ shifted by $k_1$, so $k_1 = (\text{that letter} - \texttt{E}) \bmod 26$. Repeat for $k_2, \dots, k_\ell$.
3. If $\ell$ is unknown, simply run the procedure for $\ell = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ until the recovered plaintext makes sense.

So Vigenère’s addition-mod-26 — genuinely the right primitive, as we will see — was undermined by *reusing* it at too short a period. What if the key were truly random, as long as the message, and **never reused**? That is the **one-time pad**, and in Chapter 3 we prove it is *perfectly* secure — the first security theorem of the course. The historical lesson stands: reuse of a short key over long text is where periodic ciphers die.

## 1.2.5 Rotor machines and the Enigma

The nineteenth century made ciphers electric. A **rotor machine** passes current through rotating wired substitution rotors, so each keypress applies a new substitution and the machine self-synchronizes. The **Hebern** machine (one rotor) is the archetype: the rotor *is* the secret key, wired as a substitution table, and it advances one notch per letter — pressing `C` three times might emit `T`, `S`, `K`. For a single rotor this is just a Vigenère-style period the size of the alphabet, and it was broken, soon after being advertised, by the same frequency/digram/trigram machinery.

Rotors then multiplied and complicated in an arms race against statistics, culminating in the **Enigma**: three to five rotors, each stepping at a different rate, plus a plugboard that swapped letter pairs. With four rotors the naive key count is $26^4 \approx 2^{18}$; the plugboard pushes the true figure to the region of $2^{36}$ or beyond depending on model — large, but not large by the standards of what was coming.

Its undoing was not mathematics but procedure: the machines’ operators made structural mistakes (fixed message keys, repeated message beginnings, known plaintext headers), and the British cryptographers at **Bletchley Park**, building on the Polish Cipher Bureau’s earlier work, exploited these to attack Enigma traffic cryptanalytically in World War II — an episode often credited with shortening the war. The lesson for us: a cipher is only as strong as the *protocol and key management* wrapped around it, a theme the course returns to when real systems (WEP, TLS record attacks) fail in exactly this way.

## 1.2.6 DES, AES, and the block cipher

With the mechanical age ended and computers arriving, the US government issued a request for proposals for a **Data Encryption Standard**. The IBM group’s design became **DES**: a 56-bit key, and — a conceptual jump from everything above — encrypting **64 bits at a time** (eight characters at once) rather than one character. The move from letter-level to block-level math is precisely what lets us escape frequency analysis, since no ciphertext “letter” retains a plaintext frequency to observe.

DES dominated for over two decades, but $2^{56}$ became small: by the late 1990s dedicated brute-force search was practical (the Electronic Frontier Foundation’s “Deep Crack” broke a DES key in 22 hours in 1998), and today an ordinary GPU chips through the key space in days. **DES is considered insecure and must not be used in new systems** — though, like all legacy machinery, it lingers in old ones.

Its successors, standardized in 2001 and built exactly like DES in architecture but with keys of 128 to 256 bits, are **AES**; alongside it the **eSTREAM** project (2008) certified modern stream ciphers such as **Salsa20** for high-speed software use. Chapter 4 dissects these designs; Chapter 2 explains why “encrypt 128 bits at a time with a 128-bit key” is the right shape of object for proofs.

## 1.2.7 What the tour has taught us

Read the history backward and it is the syllabus of Section 1.1:

1. **Kerckhoffs’s principle.** Assume the attacker knows the entire design; security may live only in the key. Caesar violates this in spirit; the modern formal model (threat models that give the attacker the algorithm, even the encryption oracle) institutionalizes it.
2. **Large key space ≠ security.** $26!$ and $2^{18}$ both fell. What saved block ciphers was not merely bigger keys but *invariance* — leaving the attacker’s frequency-statistics tool with nothing to grip.
3. **Reuse is the mother of attacks.** Vigenère falls to its period; one-time keys would not (proved in Chapter 3). The distinction “one-time key vs. many-time key” is the organizing axis of Parts I and II of the course.
4. **Statistics are an attacker’s birthright.** Any scheme that preserves any statistical property of plaintexts leaks; our later security definitions are all, at bottom, formal ways to say *“no efficient attacker can detect any property whatsoever beyond length.”*

That vocabulary, however, needs one more tool before we can make it precise: probability.
